Avangrid AMI Readout
What the meters are really saying
33,757 homes × 8,760 hours, 1 Sep 2025 to 31 Aug 2026. Two independent analysts (plain Claude, and Claude using the skills) worked from the same data; every number below comes from the shared pipeline in  analysis/ , and every quote and citation is machine-checked. 1,487 of the promised 35,725 customers have no data and 481 more miss over 10% of hours, so they are left out. Customers 10,001 to 20,000 come from the corrected upload.
Seven findings, in order of how much they change a decision
The system peaks on summer afternoons, set by air conditioning. The annual peak was 78.5 MW at 92F on 2 July at 5 pm; the winter peak was 56.6 MW at 7.1F on 8 February. AC homes carry 39.7 MW of weather-driven load in the summer event hours.   Electric heat is the next peak, and it is mostly invisible to the utility's lists. 3,793 homes (11.2%) heat with electricity (3,793 to 5,496 depending on threshold), and they carry 31.4% of winter peak-hour load. Each conversion adds about 3.0 kW at the winter peak, so about 7,250 more conversions (21.5% of homes) would make this system winter-peaking. In colder areas residential demand already “peaks during the winter due to the high demand for heating”  [1] , and gas “remains a primary choice for residential heating”  [2] , which is the stock that converts.   The EV list and the meters disagree, and the meters find more chargers. Only 29.3% of listed EV homes show home Level 2 charging; 50.8% show no Level 2 day at all. Meanwhile 734 homes charge at Level 2, about 522 of them beyond what the list implies. DOE's AMI report says customers rarely tell the utility, but “three customers buy a Tesla” and a transformer's load triples  [3] .   Solar is the one class the meters identify almost perfectly, and almost none of it has a battery. Against the complete solar list: 92.2% recall, 86.5% precision, plus 57 solar-shaped homes not on the list. Homes whose zeros run past dark (solar plus battery): 0. A battery program would add new storage, not reuse existing storage.   A battery's best job here is letting a home electrify without a service upgrade. An unmanaged 7.2 kW Level 2 charger breaks the 3 kVA rule at 99.3% of homes that lack one today. One IQ 10C fixes 57.2% of them; the program's two-battery setup fixes 100%. A heat pump alone stays within 3 kVA at 98.1% of homes, because those homes already peak in summer.   For pure peak relief, thermostats beat batteries on cost; batteries pay where they avoid an upgrade. Batteries at the 5,341 persistent-peak homes deliver 19.4 MW in summer and 14.8 MW in winter event hours. A thermostat setback across AC homes gives about 13.1 MW (assumption: 33% shed). On assumed prices the batteries take 23 to 33 years to pay back (assumption), against a 15-year warranty, so their case rests on avoided upgrades: to keep peaks from overloading assets, operators “must invest in excess capacity”  [4] .   About 1 in 10 meters is a seasonal home, and treating it like a normal home skews targeting and forecasts. 3,064 homes (9.1%) have a month under 15% of their biggest month, yet hold 11.5% of winter peak-hour load. The pipeline keeps them out of battery and DR targeting.

Additional data that would most improve this
The 1,487 missing customers, mostly the 1,415 IDs absent from the corrected 10,001 to 20,000 upload.
Transformer and feeder mapping: turns system-level MW into which transformers overload, which is where a battery avoids an upgrade.
Gas service flags: confirms which electric-heat homes heat with electricity only.
Charger rebates, interconnection and DMV registrations: validates the unlisted EV and solar homes.
15-minute intervals: hourly averages hide short peaks, so the 3 kVA test here is optimistic.
NYSEG/RG&E tariffs and avoided-cost values: replace every assumption below.
